# Domain-wall pulse coincidence

The apparatus uses two independent absorbing branching velocities. A common time and size measurement correlates them. Each optical flash also samples the trajectories used by the time and area meters. The single-wall dynamics belongs to the Brownian-force domain-wall model. [Dobrinevski, Le Doussal and Wiese](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.85.031105) discuss joint size and duration statistics in this model.

For the Laplace variable s conjugate to total size, the backward transform is exp(−v u), where du/dt=s−k u−Dm u². It propagates analytically between optical flashes. A flash adds its velocity load to u. Taking an infinite terminal load selects a wall dead by T. Differentiating at fixed laboratory flash times gives a wall dying at T. Starting instead from the stable root accounts for the full unconditioned evolution after the flashes. With a first trigger the other wall survives T, and with a last trigger it died earlier. Products in transform space sum both sizes. The oracle inverts each selected measure before taking the optical ratio.

The private calculation independently conditions each wall on its own lifetime. The killed square-root diffusion has the same bridge to its first zero as the radial square of a four-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck bridge. Its initial radius is √(2v/(Dm)) and its terminal radius is zero. The size transform is a quadratic tilt, changing the oscillator frequency from k/2 to √(k²/4+Dm s). Each flash is another Gaussian quadratic form at the two marked times. Integrating the second wall's lifetime on either side of T gives the same observable without applying a common-terminal extinction derivative. Independent inverse-transform grids agree within 5 × 10⁻⁹ on the 21 frozen readings. Both forms recover the elementary lifetime law at zero optical and size load. Exchanging wall labels while transporting the observed trigger identity leaves each prediction invariant.

The completed control calculates the conditional extinction, total size and exact silence probability of each flash separately. It multiplies those two probabilities, treating the flashes as separate recorded shots. The resulting absolute errors reach 0.00734, 0.00760 and 0.01007 against a tolerance of 0.004. The weaker public approximation ignores the size and extinction conditions in its mean velocity. Both controls recover the single-wall variance calibration.
