# Continuum feedback through a temporal interferometer

A phase-controlled delay returns a chiral emitter's own radiation as a two-photon drive. An atom that is still excited after the first encounter and an atom accompanied by two emitted photons can contribute to the same measured output. Radiation within a constant drive interval has structure from absorption and spontaneous emission. The whole waveform is mixed and returned. The underlying Fock-state input–output theory is developed by [Baragiola et al.](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.86.013811). The representation of delayed coherent quantum feedback by cascaded systems is described by [Grimsmo](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.115.060402).

## Exact calculation

At a common delay coordinate there are four atomic indices, one for each arm of each pass. Rotate first-pass output channels into second-pass input channels by S_{kd,jc}=T_{kj} W_j[d,c]. On a forward chronological index use the ordinary atomic operators; on an arm running backward along the delay coordinate, transpose each single-arm transfer matrix. The delay contracts emissions on a first-pass channel a with absorptions on a second-pass channel b. This gives the directional cross term −∑ba S_ba L₁a L₂b† in addition to the four free atomic generators. A selected incident test field f contributes −L₁†f−L₂†Sf and a selected outgoing test bra h contributes hSL₁+hL₂. The direct optical contraction is hSf. Atomic operators on different replicas commute. Transposition acts on the corresponding replica indices, with all laser and propagation phases preserved.

Use an independent formal variable for each incident and outgoing photon. The coefficient containing all four is its Fock matrix element. Square-free coefficients suffice, so the sparse propagation dimension is 16×3⁴. At the two outer ends, connect the four atomic transfers into their physical time order, inserting the RF pulse and the no-gap-emission vacuum propagator between passages. The final two-photon success event excludes any gap photon by excitation conservation. Divide double-occupation receiver amplitudes by √2. This calculation supplies the frozen labels.

The independent reference propagates an actual two-excitation wavefunction on fine traveling time bins. It carries two symmetric two-photon arrays for the atomic ground components and one single-photon array for the excited component. Each collision is a small, excitation-conserving oscillator–atom unitary. The two-arm temporal interferometer rotates the complete arrays at handoff, preserving the atom–radiation correlations, after which the bins collide again in their new chronology. Richardson refinement of the independent discretization checks the exact fringes and success probabilities for all 24 graded settings. The square-pulse example also has an analytical check. With Γ₀=1 and a unit-duration incident square wave, the forward bright output is g(t)=2exp(−t/2)−1 and the leftover atomic excitation is −B with B=2(1−exp(−1/2)). The square receiver after rescattering gives the overlap of g(1−t) with the routed wave. At optical phase π the returned bright probability is [B²+∫g(1−t)g(t)dt]² for chronological replay or [B²+∫g(t)²dt]²=1 for wholly reversed replay. The spectator is transmitted. All hidden geometries differ from these two limits.

## Controls

The public starter projects first-pass radiation onto the flat drive subintervals and returns every retained spatial and atomic amplitude coherently. This loses optical modes generated during the first encounter. The independent negative control propagates the entire return field accurately but inserts a measurement of whether the waveguide atom is excited at handoff, then discards its result. Both give finite readings, calibrate V=0.8436, and disagree with held-out waveguide measurements. The verifier uses only the calibration and frozen readings. `scripts/validate_photon_transfer_fringe.py` checks the analytical examples, independent propagation, and the controls.
